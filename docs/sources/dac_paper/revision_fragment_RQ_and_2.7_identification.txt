RESEARCH QUESTION (replaces the opening of the fourth paragraph of Section 1)

This article asks a narrower question than how long the response took: can
dated records of orders and of the deliveries of those same orders
distinguish a delay set by the decision to order from a delay set by the
availability of supply, and, where they cannot, what evidence would? The
distinction matters for preparedness. If delivery was limited by the
decision, ordering earlier would have brought protection earlier; if it was
limited by supply, it would not, and the remedy lies in stocks, production
capacity or access to suppliers. The two mechanisms can produce the same
interval between an order and its delivery, so the interval alone cannot
tell them apart.

2.7 Identification: decision-limited and supply-limited delay

Let an authority place orders i = 1, ..., n for one good, on dates O_i, and
let D_i be the first verified delivery of order i. Two mechanisms are
compared.

- Decision-limited (M1): D_i = O_i + k. Once ordered, the good takes a fixed
  time k to arrive, so delivery moves one-for-one with the order date.
- Supply-limited (M2): D_i = max(O_i + k, R). The good is not available to
  this buyer before a date R, set by production, allocation or export
  restrictions, so an order placed before R − k is delivered at R whatever
  its date.

For orders placed before R − k, each model has one free parameter (k or R),
so their fits can be compared directly.

Proposition 1. (i) A single paired order cannot distinguish M1 from M2.
(ii) Two or more paired orders of the same good, placed on different dates
before R − k, can: M1 predicts D_j − D_i = O_j − O_i, and M2 predicts
D_j − D_i = 0. (iii) An unpaired interval, from an order to a delivery of
goods from another source, cannot distinguish them.

Proof. (i) For any observed pair with D ≥ O, M1 fits exactly with k = D − O,
and M2 fits exactly with R = D and any k ≤ D − O. (ii) Under M1,
D_j − D_i = (O_j + k) − (O_i + k) = O_j − O_i. Under M2, both orders satisfy
O + k ≤ R, so D_i = D_j = R. The predictions differ whenever O_i ≠ O_j.
(iii) If the delivery dated D is not the delivery of the order dated O,
neither model constrains D − O. ∎

The proposition is elementary; its use lies in what it rules out. Four
consequences follow for the analysis.

First, discrimination needs orders of one good placed on different dates.
The slope of delivery date on order date is 1 under M1 and 0 under M2. Real
records, with variation from transport and partial delivery, give values in
between, and these are informative only when the order dates are spread
more widely than that variation.

Second, orders placed after R − k behave the same under both models. A
decision-limited pattern among late orders therefore shows only that any
supply constraint had lifted by then, not that there never was one.

Third, orders of different goods, or from different suppliers, may differ in
k or in R, so a difference between their lags does not test M1 against M2.
It can test a third mechanism: a delay common to the purchasing service (M0,
the same k for every good), which predicts equal lags for orders placed at
the same time.

Fourth, the counterfactual that matters for the warning paradox — how much
earlier delivery would have come had the order been placed earlier — is
O_obs − O_cf under M1, and zero under M2 for orders placed before R − k. It
is identified only once the mechanism is. Observed elapsed time, the
mechanism behind it and the avoidable part of it are therefore three
different quantities, and the record may establish the first without the
other two.

Where paired orders are too few or too close together, other records can
discriminate: a delivery date promised by the supplier at contracting, which
under M2 clusters at R regardless of the order date; production or dispatch
records; or documented export restrictions or allocation decisions that date
R directly. Section 4.2 applies the test to the Belgian orders and states
which records would settle what it leaves open.
