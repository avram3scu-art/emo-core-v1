2.8 Relation to existing approaches

Table 1 compares the profile with the closest approaches to dating and
explaining a crisis response. None of them is designed to do what
Proposition 1 requires: to link each order to the delivery of that same
order, from records, so that the dependence of delivery on order date can be
observed.

Table 1. The profile and the closest existing approaches.

1. Policy-adoption trackers (Hale et al., 2021)
   Unit/interval:   country-day; date a measure was adopted
   Dates/sources:   daily coding; no windows; one channel
   Separates M1/M2: no — neither orders nor deliveries are recorded
   Profile adds:    a delivery end point, and orders paired with deliveries

2. Time-to-event analysis of adoption (Siedschlag & Yan, 2020;
   Box-Steffensmeier & Jones, 2004)
   Unit/interval:   country; trigger to adoption, with censoring
   Dates/sources:   censoring handled; windows rarely; one event per case
   Separates M1/M2: no — the outcome is adoption, not delivery
   Profile adds:    steps after adoption; contested dates carried as readings

3. Audits of procurement programmes (National Audit Office, 2020)
   Unit/interval:   programme; volumes ordered and received by month
   Dates/sources:   channels reported separately; aggregate volumes
   Separates M1/M2: only from documents, not from the data — aggregates do
                    not link an order to its delivery
   Profile adds:    order-level pairing, so the test can be run on the record

4. Interviews with procurement staff (Morales-Contreras et al., 2021)
   Unit/interval:   organisation; perceived lead times
   Dates/sources:   not dated
   Separates M1/M2: no — perceptions, not dated events
   Profile adds:    dated records of orders and deliveries

5. Supply-chain resilience models: time to recover and time to survive
   (Simchi-Levi et al., 2015)
   Unit/interval:   node in a supply network; disruption to recovery
   Dates/sources:   parallel paths modelled; recovery times are model inputs
   Separates M1/M2: not as a test — the model assumes supply disruption and
                    estimates its effect
   Profile adds:    an empirical check, from public records, of whether
                    delivery was set by supply at all

6. Process tracing (Collier, 2011; Beach & Pedersen, 2019)
   Unit/interval:   case; evidence for and against a mechanism
   Dates/sources:   any evidence; no fixed coding
   Separates M1/M2: yes in principle, through within-case tests
   Profile adds:    a coding protocol and a specified test: which paired
                    observations eliminate which mechanism

The profile is closest to process tracing, and it is an application of it
rather than a rival. In Collier's (2011) terms, the prediction of the
decision-limited mechanism that delivery moves with the order date is a hoop
test: an explanation that fails it is eliminated, while passing it does not
confirm the explanation. The contrast between the surgical-mask and FFP2
orders of 15 and 16 March is a hoop test that a delay common to the
purchasing service fails (Section 4.3). The clustering of FFP2 deliveries is
at most a straw in the wind for a supply constraint, because a procedure
specific to FFP2 respirators passes the same test. What the profile
contributes is narrower than a new theory of crisis response. It specifies
explicitly, for public records, which observations would eliminate which
explanation, and it carries contested and imprecise dates through the test
instead of resolving them by assumption.
