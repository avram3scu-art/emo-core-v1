THE SCIENCE OF TRUTH
MATHEMATICAL TEST DESIGN

Adrian N. Avramescu

Preregistered Simulation Protocol (Draft)

============================================================
1. PURPOSE
============================================================

The objective is not to assume that The Science of Truth works.

The objective is to construct a test capable of detecting whether
a structured educational program improves students' ability to
evaluate evidence, reason critically, recognize uncertainty, and
revise conclusions when presented with new information.

We must distinguish three different questions:

A. MATHEMATICAL VALIDITY
   Are the equations and statistical procedures internally
   consistent under their stated assumptions?

B. COMPUTATIONAL VALIDITY
   Does a simulation implement the intended statistical model
   and produce the expected behavior across repeated trials?

C. EMPIRICAL VALIDITY
   Does teaching The Science of Truth actually improve students'
   reasoning compared with ordinary teaching?

A mathematical proof cannot establish an empirical educational
effect without empirical premises and evidence.

A simulation can test our statistical procedure under specified
conditions. It cannot establish that those conditions accurately
represent real classrooms.

Therefore, the first task is to test the test itself.


============================================================
2. THE CENTRAL HYPOTHESIS
============================================================

Let:

    T = structured Science of Truth curriculum
    C = ordinary curriculum or comparison condition
    Y = independently assessed reasoning performance

The primary research question is:

    Does T produce better reasoning outcomes than C?

Define the average treatment effect:

    tau = E[Y(1) - Y(0)]

where:

    Y(1) = outcome under the Science of Truth curriculum
    Y(0) = outcome under the comparison curriculum

The primary null hypothesis is:

    H0: tau = 0

The primary alternative hypothesis is:

    H1: tau != 0

A directional hypothesis could instead be preregistered:

    H0: tau <= 0
    H1: tau > 0

However, the choice between a one-sided and two-sided test must
be made before examining the results.

For the initial design, use a two-sided significance level:

    alpha = 0.05

This means that, under the null model and the assumptions of the
statistical test, the probability of a false-positive rejection
should be approximately 5%.


============================================================
3. WHAT MUST BE MEASURED?
============================================================

The primary outcome must not simply ask whether students agree
with the curriculum's principles.

That would risk measuring conformity rather than reasoning.

Instead, develop an independently assessed reasoning test
containing unfamiliar problems.

Possible assessment components include:

    1. Distinguishing observations from interpretations.
    2. Identifying unsupported conclusions.
    3. Evaluating the relevance and reliability of evidence.
    4. Recognizing uncertainty and limitations.
    5. Comparing competing explanations.
    6. Identifying confounding variables.
    7. Updating a conclusion when new evidence appears.
    8. Recognizing when evidence is insufficient to decide.

The assessment should include questions where the scientifically
appropriate answer is to reject a claim, accept a claim
provisionally, or suspend judgment.

The assessment must not reward agreement with any particular
philosophy, political position, religion, or worldview.

Before the main trial, the assessment should be piloted to
evaluate reliability, age appropriateness, and whether it measures
the intended abilities.

Whenever feasible, assessors should be blinded to each student's
group assignment.


============================================================
4. PROPOSED EXPERIMENTAL DESIGN
============================================================

The design proceeds in two stages: a pilot (feasibility) study,
followed by a cluster-randomized controlled trial whose sample
size is set from the pilot's measured values.

Simulation (Section 8) showed that the originally proposed
80 classrooms would likely be underpowered once realistic
departures from the idealized model are combined. The trial
size therefore cannot responsibly be fixed in advance.

STAGE 1: PILOT STUDY

The pilot does not test whether the curriculum works.
It estimates the quantities that determine whether a full
trial is feasible and how large it must be:

    1. Intraclass correlation of the reasoning assessment.
    2. Reliability of the assessment.
    3. Baseline/follow-up correlation.
    4. Implementation fidelity in treatment classrooms.
    5. Contamination of comparison classrooms.
    6. Attrition rate, separately in each arm.
    7. Number of classes taught per teacher.

The pilot must not be used to estimate or report the
treatment effect as evidence of benefit.

STAGE 2: MAIN TRIAL

Unit of randomization:

    The unit of randomization is the classroom if each teacher
    teaches only one participating class.

    If teachers teach more than one participating class, the
    teacher (or the school) is the unit of randomization, and
    all of that teacher's classes receive the same condition.

    Simulation showed that randomizing classrooms while ignoring
    shared teachers raised the false-positive rate from 5% to
    approximately 8%.

Sample size:

    Determined after the pilot, by simulation using the pilot's
    estimates, so that the trial has at least 80% power to
    detect the preregistered minimum educationally meaningful
    effect AFTER allowing for expected fidelity and
    contamination.

    For planning only: under the optimistic profile in
    Section 8, roughly 160 classrooms were needed. Under the
    moderate profile, more than 320 were needed.

Attrition:

    Retention procedures must be specified in advance.
    Attrition must be reported separately for each arm.

    A preregistered sensitivity analysis (for example, bounds
    on the treatment effect under plausible selective dropout)
    must accompany the primary result.

    Simulation showed that 15% attrition concentrated among
    struggling treated students produced a spurious effect of
    approximately +0.13 to +0.15 SD. A larger trial does not
    reduce this bias.

PROCEDURE

    1. Complete and report the pilot study.
    2. Fix the sample size, primary analysis and decision
       rules; preregister them.
    3. Recruit participating classrooms or teachers.
    4. Obtain appropriate institutional approval and consent.
    5. Measure baseline reasoning performance.
    6. Randomly assign units to T or C.
    7. Deliver the two instructional conditions.
    8. Record implementation fidelity, contamination and
       attendance throughout.
    9. Administer the follow-up assessment with blinded scoring.
   10. Analyze according to original assignment.
   11. Report all preregistered outcomes, attrition by arm,
       sensitivity analyses and deviations.
   12. Make anonymized data and analysis code available whenever
       ethically and legally possible.

The comparison group should receive ordinary teaching or a
carefully specified alternative program.

The study should not deliberately deprive children of necessary
educational support.


============================================================
5. STATISTICAL MODEL
============================================================

For student i in classroom j, define:

    Y_ij,0 = baseline reasoning score
    Y_ij,1 = follow-up reasoning score
    T_j    = classroom treatment assignment

A simple primary model is:

    Y_ij,1 = a + tau*T_j + b*Y_ij,0 + u_j + e_ij

where:

    a     = intercept
    tau   = treatment effect
    b     = baseline adjustment coefficient
    u_j   = classroom-level variation
    e_ij  = individual-level variation

The coefficient tau estimates the adjusted average difference
between the two instructional conditions.

Because treatment is assigned by classroom, the analysis must
account for the dependence between students in the same classroom.

Possible methods include:

    A. A prespecified mixed-effects model.
    B. A cluster-level analysis with appropriate adjustment.
    C. Randomization-based inference respecting classroom
       assignment.
    D. Cluster-robust inference with suitable small-sample
       corrections.

The final method should be selected before the trial begins.

The simple simulation below uses classroom means and an ordinary
least-squares regression. It is an initial computational check,
not the final analysis plan for a real trial.


ANALYSIS DECISIONS SUPPORTED BY SIMULATION

    Primary analysis:
        Ordinary least squares on means at the unit of
        randomization (classroom or teacher), adjusted for the
        baseline mean.

    Confirmatory analysis:
        Randomization inference on baseline-adjusted residuals,
        permuting at the unit of randomization.

    Excluded:
        Student-level regression that ignores clustering.
        In simulation its false-positive rate was 17% to 40%
        instead of 5%.


============================================================
6. INITIAL SIMULATION ASSUMPTIONS
============================================================

The first simulation investigates three possible true effects:

    tau = 0.00 standard deviations
    tau = 0.20 standard deviations
    tau = 0.30 standard deviations

Interpretation:

    0.00 = no true treatment effect
    0.20 = a small illustrative positive effect
    0.30 = a larger illustrative positive effect

These values are hypothetical. They are not claims about the
actual effectiveness of the curriculum.

Additional assumptions:

    Number of classrooms:              80
    Students per classroom:             17
    Intraclass correlation (ICC):       0.10
    Baseline/follow-up correlation:     0.60
    Significance threshold:             0.05
    Simulation repetitions:             3,000

The intraclass correlation represents the degree to which
students in the same classroom resemble one another.

The baseline/follow-up correlation represents the association
between students' initial and later scores.

The assumptions must be varied in further simulations.

NOTE:

The Section 7 script uses 17 students per classroom directly
and does not model attrition. Attrition, unequal class sizes,
teacher effects and the other departures listed in Section 9
are simulated by the additional scripts described in Section 7,
with results in Section 8.


============================================================
7. REPRODUCIBLE PYTHON SIMULATION
============================================================

Requirements:

    Python 3
    numpy
    scipy

Install dependencies with:

    python -m pip install numpy scipy

    simulate_trial.py

import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import t as student_t


def simulate_trial(
    tau,
    rng,
    n_classes=80,
    n_students=17,
    icc=0.10,
    rho=0.60
):
    """
    Simulate a simplified classroom-randomized trial.

    tau:
        True standardized treatment effect.

    rng:
        NumPy random-number generator.

    n_classes:
        Total number of randomized classrooms.

    n_students:
        Students observed per classroom.

    icc:
        Assumed classroom-level variance proportion.

    rho:
        Assumed baseline/follow-up correlation parameter.
    """

    if n_classes % 2 != 0:
        raise ValueError("n_classes must be even.")

    if not 0 <= icc < 1:
        raise ValueError("icc must be in [0, 1).")

    if not 0 <= rho < 1:
        raise ValueError("rho must be in [0, 1).")

    # Randomly assign half of the classrooms to each condition.
    treatment = np.repeat(
        [0, 1],
        n_classes // 2
    )
    rng.shuffle(treatment)

    # Baseline scores: classroom and individual components.
    class_baseline = rng.normal(size=n_classes)
    individual_baseline = rng.normal(
        size=(n_classes, n_students)
    )

    y0 = (
        np.sqrt(icc) * class_baseline[:, None]
        + np.sqrt(1 - icc) * individual_baseline
    )

    # Independent classroom and individual components for
    # follow-up noise.
    class_followup = rng.normal(size=n_classes)
    individual_followup = rng.normal(
        size=(n_classes, n_students)
    )

    noise = (
        np.sqrt(icc) * class_followup[:, None]
        + np.sqrt(1 - icc) * individual_followup
    )

    # Generate follow-up outcomes.
    y1 = (
        rho * y0
        + np.sqrt(1 - rho**2) * noise
        + tau * treatment[:, None]
    )

    # Analyze classroom means.
    baseline_mean = y0.mean(axis=1)
    followup_mean = y1.mean(axis=1)

    X = np.column_stack([
        np.ones(n_classes),
        treatment,
        baseline_mean
    ])

    beta = np.linalg.solve(
        X.T @ X,
        X.T @ followup_mean
    )

    residual = followup_mean - X @ beta

    df = n_classes - X.shape[1]

    variance = (residual @ residual) / df

    covariance = variance * np.linalg.inv(X.T @ X)

    se = np.sqrt(covariance[1, 1])

    statistic = beta[1] / se

    p_value = 2 * student_t.sf(
        abs(statistic),
        df
    )

    return beta[1], p_value


def run_simulation(
    repetitions=3000,
    effects=(0.00, 0.20, 0.30),
    seed=20261026
):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)

    print("THE SCIENCE OF TRUTH")
    print("INITIAL SIMULATION RESULTS")
    print("----------------------------------------")

    for tau in effects:

        results = [
            simulate_trial(tau, rng)
            for _ in range(repetitions)
        ]

        estimates = np.array([
            result[0] for result in results
        ])

        significant = np.array([
            result[1] < 0.05
            for result in results
        ])

        rejection_rate = significant.mean()

        monte_carlo_se = np.sqrt(
            rejection_rate
            * (1 - rejection_rate)
            / repetitions
        )

        print(
            f"True effect: {tau:.2f} SD | "
            f"Rejection rate: {rejection_rate:.4f} | "
            f"Monte Carlo SE: {monte_carlo_se:.4f} | "
            f"Mean estimate: {estimates.mean():.4f}"
        )


if __name__ == "__main__":
    run_simulation()

============================================================
8. SIMULATION RESULTS
============================================================

All results are conditional on modelled assumptions. They test
the statistical procedure, not the curriculum. Scripts are
described in Section 7 (seeds 20261026 and 20261027).

8.1 REPRODUCTION OF THE INITIAL SIMULATION

The Section 7 script reproduced its reported results exactly:

    True effect     Rejection rate     Mean estimate
    0.00 SD             4.53%              0.002
    0.20 SD            80.50%              0.201
    0.30 SD            98.73%              0.301

This calibration is close to guaranteed by construction:
normal errors and equal cluster sizes analyzed by ordinary least
squares on cluster means is the setting in which the t-test is
exact. It confirms the code, not the design.

8.2 SINGLE DEPARTURES (2,000 trials per condition)

False-positive control:
    The primary analysis stayed near 5% under varied ICC and
    rho, random and baseline-related attrition, unequal class
    sizes, heavy-tailed scores, measurement error, and 20
    classrooms.

Power at 0.20 SD fell from 82% to:

    ICC 0.20                     60%
    rho 0.40                     71%
    Assessment reliability 0.60  67%
    Fidelity 75%                 56%
    Fidelity 50%                 29%
    Contamination 40%            63%

Power at 0.10 SD under ideal conditions: 31%.

Failures:

    Differential attrition (low-outcome treated students leave):
        15%: bias +0.13 SD, false-positive rate 47%
        25%: bias +0.20 SD, false-positive rate 77%

    Two classes per teacher, analyzed at classroom level:
        false-positive rate 8.2%.
        Analysis at teacher level restored 4.1%, with power
        reduced to 49%.

    Student-level analysis ignoring clustering:
        false-positive rate 17% to 40%.

8.3 COMBINED DEPARTURES (1,000 trials per condition)

Three planning profiles combined several departures.
Fidelity and contamination reduce a true 0.20 SD effect to the
delivered intention-to-treat effect shown.

    Profile       Delivered   80 cl.  120   160   240   320
    Optimistic    0.17        55%     73%   87%   96%   99%
    Moderate      0.14        22%     33%   43%   60%   72%
    Pessimistic   0.11        14%     16%   22%   31%   38%

    Optimistic:  ICC .10, rho .6, reliability .8, fidelity 90%,
                 contamination 10%, attrition 15%
    Moderate:    ICC .15, rho .5, reliability .7, fidelity 80%,
                 contamination 20%, attrition 15%,
                 2 classes per teacher
    Pessimistic: ICC .20, rho .4, reliability .6, fidelity 70%,
                 contamination 30%, attrition 20%,
                 2 classes per teacher

These profile values are planning assumptions, not estimates
from the literature or from classrooms.

The false-positive rate remained between 4% and 7%.
The optimistic profile initially ran at 6-7%. A check with
10,000 trials per condition (seed 20261028), at 80 and 160
classrooms, with and without unequal sizes and attrition, gave
4.6% to 5.4% for every analysis. The earlier excess was Monte
Carlo noise.

With 15% differential attrition added to the moderate profile,
the estimated effect of a curriculum with no true effect was
about +0.15 SD at both 80 and 320 classrooms. The false-positive
rate rose from 30% to 79% as the trial grew.

INTERPRETATION

    1. The test is well calibrated under most departures.
    2. The 80-classroom design is underpowered under all but
       the most optimistic assumptions.
    3. Fidelity, contamination and assessment reliability
       determine power more cheaply than added classrooms.
    4. Differential attrition is the principal threat to
       validity, and it cannot be cured by sample size.


============================================================
9. WHAT MUST BE TESTED NEXT?
============================================================

STATUS: Items A-G and I-K have been simulated (Section 8).
Item H (fidelity) was simulated as a parameter, but recording
fidelity in real classrooms remains an empirical task.
The items are retained here as the checklist the design
must continue to satisfy.

The initial simulation is insufficient for a definitive design.

The next simulation must investigate the following conditions.

A. FALSE-POSITIVE CONTROL

Test whether the false-positive rate remains close to 5% when
the true effect is zero.

Repeat under different assumptions and analysis methods.

B. STATISTICAL POWER

Estimate the probability of detecting effects of:

    0.00 SD
    0.10 SD
    0.20 SD
    0.30 SD

C. INTRACLASS CORRELATION

Investigate:

    ICC = 0.05
    ICC = 0.10
    ICC = 0.20

D. BASELINE/FOLLOW-UP CORRELATION

Investigate:

    rho = 0.40
    rho = 0.60
    rho = 0.80

E. ATTRITION

Begin with 20 students per classroom and explicitly simulate
follow-up loss of:

    5%
    15%
    25%

Investigate both random attrition and attrition related to
baseline scores or treatment assignment.

Differential attrition may bias the treatment estimate and
must not be treated merely as a reduction in sample size.

F. UNEQUAL CLASSROOM SIZES

Real classrooms will not all have the same number of students.

Test whether the analysis remains reliable when classroom sizes
vary substantially.

G. TEACHER EFFECTS

Teachers differ in experience, instructional skill, enthusiasm,
and adherence to the curriculum.

The simulation should distinguish teacher variation from
classroom variation where the design permits it.

H. IMPLEMENTATION FIDELITY

A curriculum may fail because its underlying theory is incorrect,
because the teaching materials are inadequate, or because the
program is not delivered as intended.

Record implementation fidelity so these possibilities can be
examined rather than conflated.

I. CONTAMINATION

Students or teachers in the comparison group may encounter
elements of the intervention.

Simulate contamination and examine its effect on estimates.

J. OUTCOME MEASUREMENT

The assessment may be noisy, unreliable, or inadvertently biased
toward the intervention.

Investigate the consequences of measurement error and assess
whether independent scoring improves reliability.

K. ANALYSIS ROBUSTNESS

Compare the prespecified primary method with suitable alternatives,
including randomization-based inference and appropriate
mixed-effects or cluster-adjusted methods.

Alternative analyses should diagnose robustness, not be used
to select whichever result appears most favorable.


============================================================
10. PREREGISTRATION AND DECISION RULES
============================================================

Before collecting outcome data, publish or timestamp:

    1. The research question.
    2. The primary and secondary outcomes.
    3. The intervention and comparison conditions.
    4. The randomization procedure.
    5. The statistical analysis plan.
    6. The minimum educationally meaningful effect.
    7. The planned sample size and its assumptions.
    8. The treatment of missing data.
    9. The exclusion criteria.
   10. The rules for reporting deviations.

Define the minimum educationally meaningful effect before
examining the results.

Statistical significance alone does not establish educational
importance.

The results should be interpreted using effect estimates,
confidence intervals, assessment quality, implementation data,
and the prespecified decision criteria.

POSSIBLE OUTCOMES

OUTCOME 1: EVIDENCE OF BENEFIT

The intervention produces a sufficiently precise positive effect
that meets the preregistered criteria for educational importance.

Conclusion:

    The results support further evaluation of the curriculum.

Do not conclude that the program works for every age, school,
subject, or educational context.

OUTCOME 2: INCONCLUSIVE EVIDENCE

The estimated effect is uncertain, or the confidence interval
includes both negligible and meaningful effects.

Conclusion:

    The study does not establish whether the intervention
    produces a meaningful benefit.

A larger or improved study may be justified.

OUTCOME 3: EVIDENCE AGAINST A MEANINGFUL BENEFIT

The confidence interval is sufficiently narrow to exclude the
preregistered minimum meaningful benefit.

Conclusion:

    The results count against the claim that the intervention
    achieves that level of benefit under the tested conditions.

OUTCOME 4: EVIDENCE OF HARM OR UNINTENDED CONSEQUENCES

The intervention worsens outcomes or creates identifiable
problems, such as reduced openness to correction or increased
overconfidence.

Conclusion:

    Investigate the cause, report the finding, and revise or
    discontinue the intervention as appropriate.

No outcome should be reinterpreted merely to protect the original
proposal.


============================================================
11. WHAT WOULD COUNT AS A MATHEMATICAL PROOF?
============================================================

We must be precise about the word "proof."

MATHEMATICS CAN PROVE:

    - Algebraic properties of a specified model.
    - Consequences that logically follow from stated assumptions.
    - Certain properties of an estimator under stated conditions.
    - Formal bounds or guarantees when their assumptions hold.

COMPUTATIONAL SIMULATION CAN INVESTIGATE:

    - Whether code behaves as intended.
    - Approximate false-positive rates under simulated conditions.
    - Approximate power under specified effect sizes.
    - Bias, coverage, and robustness across modeled scenarios.

EMPIRICAL RESEARCH CAN INVESTIGATE:

    - Whether students actually improve.
    - Whether improvements persist over time.
    - Whether the program generalizes across classrooms.
    - Whether the benefits justify the costs.
    - Whether unintended effects occur.

REPLICATION CAN INVESTIGATE:

    - Whether independent researchers obtain compatible results.
    - Whether findings persist in different populations and settings.

None of these methods substitutes completely for the others.


============================================================
12. FINAL POSITION
============================================================

The Science of Truth is currently a proposed educational
framework, not an experimentally established educational
intervention.

Simulation has tested the proposed test. Its findings are:

    The planned analysis controls false positives under most
    modelled conditions.

    An 80-classroom trial would most likely end inconclusive
    under realistic conditions.

    Selective attrition could produce an apparent benefit where
    none exists, and a larger trial would make that error more
    likely to reach significance, not less.

These are findings about the study design. They say nothing
about whether the curriculum works.

The next steps are, in order:

    1. Specify the curriculum clearly enough that another
       educator can implement it without depending on the
       founder's personal interpretation.

    2. Develop and pilot the reasoning assessment, and establish
       its reliability.

    3. Conduct a pilot study to estimate ICC, fidelity,
       contamination and attrition by arm.

    4. Fix the trial size by simulation using the pilot
       estimates.

    5. Have the design reviewed independently and preregister it
       before any outcome data are collected.

The final principle is unchanged:

    We must not design a test that protects the idea.

    We must design a test that gives the idea a fair opportunity
    to succeed or fail.

    If the evidence contradicts the proposal, revise the proposal.

    If the evidence supports it, replicate the result.

    If the evidence remains insufficient, admit that we do not
    yet know.

The simulations have already applied this principle once: they
showed that the original design could not deliver a fair test,
and the design has been revised accordingly.
